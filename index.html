About

Fifteen years in Indonesia's seas

Since 2013 I have followed the reef manta rays of Raja Ampat. The work has grown to include whale sharks, leopard sharks, walking sharks and oceanic manta rays, in Indonesia and New Zealand.

My research combines photo-identification, drones, acoustic and satellite telemetry, and network analysis to answer practical questions: where animals go, how long they stay, how populations change, and which habitats matter most. Those answers feed directly into the design and management of marine protected areas in Indonesia and beyond.

My PhD at the University of Auckland examined the movement ecology, population dynamics and conservation of reef manta rays in Raja Ampat. Today I am Lead Conservation Scientist at the Elasmobranch Institute Indonesia, which I co-founded.

Key findings

What the data showed

The world's first confirmed reef manta ray nursery

Eight years of monitoring confirmed that juvenile Mobula alfredi grow up in Wayag Lagoon, northern Raja Ampat. The finding helped shape the rezoning of the Waigeo Barat MPA to protect the lagoon.

Front. Mar. Sci. 2022 →

Manta populations that are growing

While reef manta rays decline globally, photo-identified populations in two Raja Ampat MPAs are increasing, a rare conservation success built on local protection.

Front. Mar. Sci. 2022 →

Measuring mantas from the air

I developed a non-invasive method that uses small drones and photogrammetry to measure the body size of free-swimming manta rays accurately.

Research

Five research programs

Each program applies the same population and movement framework to a different species and seascape, asking the questions managers need answered: how many animals there are, whether numbers are changing, where they go, and whether protected areas cover the places that matter.

Reef manta rays

Mobula alfredi

Raja Ampat · expanding across Indonesia

Flagship program

My flagship program tracks the reef manta rays of Raja Ampat: population size and trends, survival, site fidelity at cleaning stations and feeding areas, and movement between sites and marine protected areas.

Indonesia gave manta rays full national protection in 2014, so this long-term dataset is a rare chance to measure how a protected population responds over time. The program is now expanding to manta populations across Indonesia, to show how they connect and where the main threats remain.

Whale sharks

Rhincodon typus

Indonesia

Indonesia hosts several whale shark aggregations, many linked to lift-net (bagan) fisheries or to growing tourism. This program uses photo-identification to estimate how many individuals visit each site and how often they return, and telemetry to follow where they go between visits.

The results support sustainable tourism guidelines and help reduce risks from fisheries interactions and vessel strikes.

Indo-Pacific leopard sharks

Stegostoma tigrinum

Derawan Islands, East Kalimantan

Project under way

The leopard shark is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List, and its populations have declined across much of its range. This project estimates local abundance in the Derawan Islands, identifies important habitats, and measures how far individuals move.

The findings will guide protection of the species within the Berau marine area and inform regional efforts, including potential future reintroduction programs elsewhere.

Oceanic manta rays

Mobula birostris

Northern New Zealand · Bird's Head Seascape

New in New Zealand

Oceanic manta rays visit northern New Zealand seasonally, yet little is known about these populations. This project applies the methods developed in Indonesia to a temperate setting: how many oceanic mantas use New Zealand waters, when they arrive, how long they stay, and how they move through the wider South Pacific.

It also builds collaboration between Indonesian and New Zealand researchers on a globally threatened species, and draws on our earlier oceanic manta work in Indonesia.

Epaulette (walking) sharks

Hemiscyllium spp.

Raja Ampat · Bird's Head Seascape

Pew Fellowship 2025–2028

The Bird's Head Seascape is home to several species of walking sharks, some found nowhere else on Earth. They walk across shallow reefs on their fins and disperse very little, so local populations are vulnerable to habitat loss and fishing.

This project studies their abundance, home ranges and movements in Raja Ampat and the wider Bird's Head region. It provides the baseline needed to protect these endemic species, which have also become a draw for night-dive tourism.

Methods

Photo-identification

Lifelong natural markings, such as manta belly spots and the spots of leopard and whale sharks, build catalogues of known individuals from researcher, dive operator and citizen scientist photos.

Mark–recapture modelling

Repeat sightings feed models that estimate population size, survival, residency and trends, the numbers managers need to judge whether protection works.

Acoustic telemetry

Receiver arrays at cleaning stations, feeding grounds and reefs reveal site fidelity, daily and seasonal movements, and connectivity between protected areas.

Satellite telemetry

Tags on wide-ranging species show long-distance movements, diving behaviour and open-ocean habitat use beyond the reach of receivers.

Field surveys

In-water surveys record sex, maturity, injuries, behaviour and habitat use, adding context such as fishing-gear injuries or distinctive colour forms.

The lab

Elasmobranch Institute Indonesia

Sharks and rays are among the most heavily exploited and least understood marine animals in Indonesia. I co-founded the Elasmobranch Institute Indonesia, and have led its conservation science since 2024, to build the scientific evidence the country needs to manage them.

Our starting point is that species which reproduce slowly, travel widely and depend on particular habitats need conservation built on solid, site-specific data. We work with local communities, government agencies, dive operators and conservation partners so that findings become management action: where to place and zone protected areas, how to manage wildlife tourism, and how to measure whether national protection is working.

We favour long time series over single field seasons. Community rangers and dive operators contribute sightings and fieldwork, which keeps monitoring going for years and builds local ownership of the results. We are also training the next generation of Indonesian shark and ray scientists. Students and researchers interested in joining or collaborating are welcome to get in touch.
